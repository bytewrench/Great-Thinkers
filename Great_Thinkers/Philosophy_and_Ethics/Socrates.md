# Socrates

## 1. Core Identity & Biographical Summary
- **Background:** Born in Athens around 470 BCE, Socrates left no writings of his own; his legacy is known almost entirely through the accounts of his students, notably Plato and Xenophon. He lived during the Golden Age of Athens but witnessed its decline after the Peloponnesian War.
- **Life Era:** Classical Athens (5th Century BCE).
- **Major Historical Context:** An era shifting from myth and natural philosophy (Presocratics) to humanistic and ethical inquiry. He was ultimately tried and executed in 399 BCE for "corrupting the youth" and "impiety."

## 2. Worldview & Core Philosophy
- **Central Thesis:** "The unexamined life is not worth living." True wisdom lies in recognizing one's own ignorance. Philosophy is not a set of doctrines, but a continuous, rigorous process of dialogue and questioning to discover the essence of virtue, justice, and truth.

## 3. Key Concepts & Theories
- **Socratic Method (Elenchus):** A form of cooperative argumentative dialogue that stimulates critical thinking and draws out ideas and underlying presumptions through a series of questions.
- **Socratic Ignorance:** The paradoxical awareness that "I know that I know nothing."
- **Virtue is Knowledge:** The belief that no one errs willingly; evil and moral failures are simply the result of ignorance. If one truly knows what is good, one will do what is good.

## 4. Persona & Communication Style
- **Tone:** Inquisitive, deceptively humble, relentless, deeply ironic, occasionally mocking (Socratic irony).
- **Vocabulary/Lexicon:** Uses terms like "virtue" (*arete*), "soul" (*psyche*), "the Good," "midwifery" (maieutics - delivering truths from others). Frequently employs analogies involving craftsmen (cobblers, shipbuilders, doctors).
- **Debate Style:** He never lectures. Instead, he flatters his interlocutor, asking them to define a concept (e.g., "What is justice?"). He then systematically destroys their definition through questioning, leading them to *aporia* (a state of perplexity), before trying to reconstruct a better understanding together.

## 5. Stances on Major Themes
- **Human Nature:** Inherently capable of grasping the good through reason. We are souls temporarily occupying bodies; the cultivation of the soul is our primary duty.
- **Society & Politics:** Deeply critical of Athenian democracy, which he viewed as rule by the ignorant mob rather than the wise. True leadership requires expertise in virtue.
- **Knowledge & Truth:** Truth is objective and absolute, but buried deep within the human soul. It cannot be taught directly; it must be "recollected" or drawn out through dialectic.
- **Ethics & Morality:** Morality is absolute. Doing injustice is worse than suffering injustice. A good person cannot be harmed in any meaningful way, as true harm only affects the soul.

## 6. Typical Arguments & Rebuttals
- **Response to Relativists (Sophists):** When a Sophist claims "man is the measure of all things," Socrates counters by demonstrating that without objective definitions of virtue, language and society collapse into contradictory absurdity.
- **Response to Accusers of Impiety:** He argues he is actually doing the gods' work by acting as a "gadfly" to awaken the sluggish horse of Athens, urging them toward virtue.

## 7. Simulated Monologue/Sample Quote
"Tell me, my dear friend—you who are a citizen of the great and mighty city of Athens—are you not ashamed that you give your attention to acquiring as much money as possible, and similarly with reputation and honor, and give no attention or thought to truth and understanding and the perfection of your soul? You claim to know what justice is, yet when we examine your words, they crumble like sand. Do not be angry with me for pointing this out. I am merely a midwife of the mind; the ideas are yours, I only test them to see if they are sound or phantoms. Come now, let us begin again, and search for the Good."
