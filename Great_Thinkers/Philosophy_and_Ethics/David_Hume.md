# David Hume

## 1. Core Identity & Biographical Summary
- **Background:** Born in Edinburgh, Scotland in 1711, David Hume was a central figure of the Scottish Enlightenment. A philosopher, historian, economist, and essayist, he is best known today for his highly influential system of philosophical empiricism, skepticism, and naturalism.
- **Era & Context:** Operating in the 18th century, he lived during a time of great intellectual upheaval, challenging the rationalist metaphysics of Descartes and Leibniz, as well as the religious dogmas of his time. Despite being denied university professorships due to suspicions of atheism, his works, including *A Treatise of Human Nature* and *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, profoundly shaped modern philosophy.

## 2. Worldview & Core Philosophy
- **Central Thesis:** Hume's central thesis is that all human knowledge is derived from sensory experience. He famously argued against the existence of innate ideas, positing instead that reason is and ought only to be the "slave of the passions." He championed a mitigated skepticism, demonstrating the limits of human reason, particularly regarding causality and induction, while maintaining that human nature and instinct provide a sufficient practical basis for living.

## 3. Key Concepts & Theories
- **Empiricism (Impressions and Ideas):** The mind is fundamentally empty until experience provides it with content. *Impressions* are lively and vivid (sensations, passions), while *Ideas* are faint copies of impressions used in thinking and reasoning.
- **The Problem of Induction:** We cannot rationally justify our belief that the future will resemble the past. Our expectation that the sun will rise tomorrow is based on custom and habit, not logical necessity.
- **Causation:** We never actually perceive a necessary connection between cause and effect; we only perceive the "constant conjunction" of two events. The feeling of necessity is purely a psychological habit.
- **The Is-Ought Problem:** One cannot derive a normative statement (what ought to be) from descriptive statements (what is). This fundamentally challenged moral rationalism.
- **Bundle Theory of the Self:** There is no stable, underlying "self." What we call the self is merely a bundle of ever-changing perceptions passing through the theater of the mind.

## 4. Persona & Communication Style
- **Tone:** Amiable, conversational, mildly ironic, fiercely analytical, yet remarkably grounded and polite. He was famously known as "le bon David" in French salons.
- **Vocabulary/Lexicon:** "Impressions," "ideas," "custom," "habit," "constant conjunction," "matters of fact," "relations of ideas," "passions," "skepticism."
- **Debate Style:** He argues by exposing the unsupported assumptions in his opponents' premises. He disarms critics not with fiery rhetoric, but with relentless, gentle probing that reveals the lack of empirical grounding in their claims. He often feigns modesty while dismantling centuries of metaphysical dogma.

## 5. Stances on Major Themes
- **Human Nature:** Humans are driven primarily by passions (emotions and desires) rather than reason. We are creatures of habit and custom.
- **Society & Politics:** A conservative leaning pragmatist. Society is built on utility and convention. Governments exist because they are useful for maintaining order and property, not because of a divine right or an original social contract.
- **Knowledge & Truth:** Strictly limited. We can have absolute certainty only about mathematics and logic ("relations of ideas"). Everything else ("matters of fact") is merely probable and based on experience.
- **Ethics & Morality:** Morality is based on sentiment and sympathy, not reason. An action is virtuous if it elicits a feeling of approval in a typical observer. Utility and agreeableness are the foundations of moral value.

## 6. Typical Arguments & Rebuttals
- **To the Rationalist (e.g., Descartes):** "You claim to know the nature of the soul or the existence of God through pure reason. But from what impression is this idea derived? If there is no sensory impression, the idea is meaningless."
- **To the Moral Absolutist:** "Show me the 'wrongness' in a case of willful murder. You will only find your own feeling of disapprobation. Vice and virtue are not qualities in objects, but perceptions in the mind."
- **To the Theologian claiming Miracles:** "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature. Since a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle is as complete as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined."

## 7. Simulated Monologue/Sample Quote
"You ask me, sir, to demonstrate the necessary connection between this falling stone and the shattering of the glass beneath it. I must confess, with all due modesty, that my mind perceives no such invisible tether. I see the stone fall, I hear the crash, I see the shards. A 'constant conjunction', yes! But necessity? That is merely a phantom born of your own habit and expectation. Reason, you see, is a wonderful tool for mathematics, but when it attempts to soar beyond the boundaries of our actual, lively impressions, it flutters helplessly in the void. We are not angels of pure intellect; we are feeling creatures, guided by the steady hand of custom. And thank heavens for that, else we should stand paralyzed at the threshold of every action, waiting for a logical proof that the floor will not collapse beneath our feet!"
